A note on the data behind the demo

There is no live customer behind this design, so the demo runs on generated history. That history comes from a separate simulator the application cannot see: it holds the hidden truth about demand, vendor behaviour and price drift, and emits only the transaction records such a business would have left behind.

The forecast is then measured on a stretch of time it was never shown, and always against a simple baseline. If it cannot beat that baseline, the baseline is used and the accuracy screen says so. This is what keeps the intelligence honest rather than circular.

The target business is a cloud kitchen or small restaurant group running two to four outlets. Everything here is sized for that, and would need rethinking at ten times the scale.

Three roles, not five

A three‑outlet kitchen has an owner, a manager per outlet, and a part‑time accountant. Modelling a separate procurement officer and inventory officer would be modelling an organisation chart that does not exist.

RoleSeesCan do
OwnerAll outletsEverything — vendors, materials, recipes, approvals, costs and margins, analytics, AI advice, users, alert settings.
Outlet managerOwn outlet onlyRaise a purchase order within a spend limit, receive goods, record wastage, run a stock count, import sales, request a transfer. Cannot see other outlets or record payments.
AccountsAll outlets, money onlyView bills, record payments, mark a bill disputed, view spend reports. Cannot touch stock, orders or recipes.
Vendors do not log in

In this segment vendors work on WhatsApp, not portals, and a login for them would double the security surface for no real gain. Instead the system produces a purchase‑order PDF and a ready‑to‑send WhatsApp message that a person presses send on. A simple link‑based confirmation page can come later.

3How work flows

The purchase loop — whenever something is needed

1
A need appears — either the AI recommends it, or a manager raises it directly.
2
A purchase order is drafted with a suggested vendor, a quantity rounded to pack size, and a by‑when date.
3
Approved by the owner, or auto‑approved if inside the manager’s spend limit.
4
Sent as a PDF by email, and as a prefilled WhatsApp message a person presses send on.
5
Goods arrive — possibly partly, possibly with rejects. Each receipt is recorded separately against the order.
6
Stock goes up automatically, as new entries in the movement ledger.
7
The bill is attached and its due date is computed as delivery date plus the vendor’s agreed credit days.
8
Payment is recorded, in full or in part, and the vendor’s performance figures update on their own.

The consumption loop — every day, and this is the engine

1
Sales are imported — dish, quantity, outlet, date — from a CSV or Excel export, or typed in.
2
Recipes are applied to convert dishes into raw materials used.
3
Stock goes down as consumption entries in the same ledger.
4
Usage rate, cover and stockout risk are recalculated per material, per outlet.

The reconcile loop — weekly, and this is the truth check

1
Someone counts the shelf and enters the real quantity.
2
The difference is recorded against what the ledger expected — never by editing history, always as a new adjustment entry.
3
That gap is the real insight. Expected usage minus actual usage is wastage, over‑portioning, theft, or a recipe that no longer matches the kitchen. Tracked per material, it is often worth more than the forecast.

The nightly loop — unattended, at 2am

refresh analytics → run 14-day forecasts → recompute reorder points and safety stock → find deadstock and overstock → score vendors → check whether another outlet has spare stock → write tomorrow's recommendations, each with its reasons → raise alerts

The most important decision in this schema

There is no current‑stock column anywhere. Stock on hand is always the sum of every movement for that material at that outlet, and movements are only ever added — a correction is a new entry, never an edit. Every inventory system that stores a quantity eventually drifts out of step with its own history. This one cannot, by construction.

How it fits together, in plain words

  • A vendor supplies many materials, each at a rate valid between two dates — so price history is a fact, not a guess.
  • A menu item is made of materials through its recipe. This is the link that turns sales into consumption.
  • A purchase order has lines; receiving it creates one or more receipts, which is how part delivery works naturally. Receipts, sales and counts all add entries to the ledger.

5How the AI works

Most of what is sold as “AI” in this space is arithmetic. Being clear about which part is which is what makes the system trustworthy.

LAYER 1
Plain code — no model

Stock balances, order totals, bill due dates, outstanding amounts, ageing buckets, order status. Exact by requirement. A model here would be a bug.

LAYER 2
Analytics — formulas, not machine learning

Average daily usage and how erratic it is. Real vendor lead time, taken at the 85th percentile rather than the average, because you plan for the bad week. Delay rate, fill rate, reject rate, price drift. Days of cover and days to stockout. Deadstock is simply no outward movement for N days while stock remains; overstock is cover far beyond what shelf life or lead time justifies.

Reorder point = average daily usage × lead time at the 85th percentile + safety stock, where safety stock grows with demand variability. This is standard inventory theory, and calling it a formula rather than AI is a strength: it can be audited line by line.

LAYER 3
Forecast — the only genuine model

Fourteen‑day demand per material, per outlet. Candidates in order of cost: same weekday last week as the baseline, a weekday‑weighted moving average, a method built for items used only twice a week, and gradient boosting on calendar features. Each is tested on time it has never seen and the winner is chosen per material. If nothing beats the baseline, the baseline is used and the accuracy screen says so.

The forecast returns a range, not just a number, and that uncertainty feeds straight into safety stock — so a material the system understands poorly automatically gets a bigger buffer.

LAYER 4
Claude — explains and answers, never calculates

Explaining: it receives the already‑computed numbers and writes one sentence. Every figure in its output is checked against the figures it was given; if a number appears that was not supplied, the system falls back to a fixed template.

Answering: typed questions are handled by calling a fixed set of read‑only functions — low stock, deadstock, pending payments, vendor performance, spend, price history, explain a recommendation. Claude picks which to call; the backend runs the query. It never writes SQL, which keeps one outlet’s data out of another’s answers and makes wrong‑but‑plausible totals impossible.

6One recommendation, end to end

computed by layers 2 and 3: paneer, Indiranagar on hand 4.2 kg used 6.8 kg/day runs out in 0.6 days vendor lead time (85th percentile) 2 days suggested 22 kg from Sri Dairy alternative Milk Mart, 6% cheaper, late on 38% of orders value at risk Rs 6,100
What Claude turns that into

“Paneer at Indiranagar will run out tomorrow — 4.2 kg left against 6.8 kg used per day. Order 22 kg from Sri Dairy today; their lead time runs to 2 days at worst. Milk Mart is 6% cheaper but late on 38% of orders, which is why it is not the pick.”

Note what happened: the decision and every number came from layers 2 and 3. Claude only chose the words. The vendor was not picked for being cheapest either — rate, reliability, fill rate, quality and credit terms are combined into a weighted score whose contributions are shown on screen. If the owner accepts the card, it becomes a purchase order that remembers which recommendation created it, so the advice can be judged later.
