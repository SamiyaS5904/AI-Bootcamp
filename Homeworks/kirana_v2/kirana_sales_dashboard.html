Sales and cash are not the same thing

A sale happens when goods leave the shop. Cash happens when the customer pays. Udhaar splits the two apart — sometimes by months. This is how a shop can look busy all day and still have an empty cash box at night. Always ask two questions, not one: how much did I sell, and how much did I actually receive?

What this data cannot tell us

Stated plainly, because a confident answer here would be invented rather than measured.

  • Profit, or any margin. The file records the selling price only. There is no purchase price, so nothing in this study says what the shop earns — only what it sells.
  • Stockouts, overstock and dead stock. There is no stock column at all. A day with no atta sold could mean nobody wanted atta, or it could mean there was none. These two are indistinguishable here, so no inventory claim is made anywhere on this page.
  • Peak hours, and therefore staffing. Bills carry a date but no time. Any "the shop is busiest at 6pm" claim would be guesswork.
  • What customers buy together. Every bill has exactly one item — 1.0 items per bill across all 105 of them. There are no baskets in this data, so cross-selling cannot be studied at all.
  • How most people paid. The payment column mixes method with status, so for 68 of 105 bills it is impossible to say whether cash or UPI came in.
  • Anything seasonal. Two months, one of them cut short on 28 Sep 2026. Far too short to read festivals, seasons or a trend.

Problems the data reveals

Flags

Four things the register reveals about the business, in the order I would fix them. The first is worth real money and is fixable this week.

01 ₹4,680 of udhaar, with no due date and no way to chase it

10.3% of sales · 14 bills · 6 people · ≈4.9 days of trade

The shop has already paid a supplier for these goods and the money has not come back. The register has no due-date column, no repayment column and no running balance, so there is no way to tell what has quietly been settled and what is lost. Worse, there is not a single part-payment anywhere in the file — udhaar here is all-or-nothing, which is the hardest possible thing to ask a neighbour for.

It is also concentrated in exactly the wrong goods: ₹3,690 of the ₹4,680 is atta and rice — 78.8% of the credit book sitting in the two products that carry the shop. And the single largest item is bill 5040: 4 bags of Atta 10kg to Sukhwinder Kaur on one day, ₹1,680 — 35.9% of all the money owed, in one transaction.

What to do. Start a real udhaar book this week with six columns: date, name, amount, due date, date paid, balance left. Cap any single credit bill at ₹500 — that one rule alone would have stopped the ₹1,680 bill. Allow part-payments, because ₹200 against ₹1,680 is a conversation and ₹1,680 is a confrontation. Take cash on atta and rice.

02 The register cannot be trusted as written

Book says ₹44,014 · truth is ₹45,540 · understated by ₹1,526 (3.4%)

One bill (5016) is entered twice, adding ₹700 of revenue that never happened. 8 bills have no amount written and 4 have no quantity. 28 amounts are written as text — Rs 420, 700/-, ₹1950 — so they cannot be added up without cleaning. Unpaid is recorded as an empty box 9 times and as a literal zero 5 times, which are the same thing written two ways.

And the date is written 5 different ways in one column, including both day-first and month-first on the same page — which for 2 bills (5036, 5096) makes the true date genuinely unknowable. The day column is worse: checked against a real 2026 calendar it is wrong on 81.0% of rows, claiming 35 Sundays where the dates give 19. One of those two columns is fiction, and it is not the dates.

What to do. None of this needs money — it needs the choice to be taken away from whoever is writing. One date format, one spelling per product, never an empty box. On paper that means ruling the columns first. Better, stop writing the fields at all and let a phone fill them in, which is what the tool in the last section does.

03 Two products carry 71.6% of the shop

Atta ₹19,740 (43.3%) + Rice ₹12,870 (28.3%)

Atta alone is 43.3% of everything the shop earns, from 24 bills. A supplier raising the price of a 10kg bag, or the shop next door pricing it ₹20 lower, moves nearly half this business. The bottom four products together — sugar, dal, biscuits and salt — are under 6.1%.

What to do. These two lines need checking daily and their local price watched monthly. Over the next few months, deliberately grow one or two mid-priced lines so the shop is not resting this hard on a single bag.

04 The till cannot be reconciled

Payment method unknown on 68 of 105 bills

The payment column mixes two completely different questions. Cash is a method. Paid, Done, Pending, Udhaar, Khata, Credit are states. They sit in the same column, in 9 different spellings. Only 23 bills actually say how the money arrived, so the shop cannot answer "how much cash should be in the box tonight?" — which is the one question a daily count is supposed to settle.

What to do. Split it into two questions that each have one answer: paid or udhaar? and, if paid, cash or UPI? Two taps, and the till becomes countable.

Evidence, interpretation, action

Insights & inferences

Each finding is split into three separate layers: what the data literally shows, what I think that means about the business, and what the owner could actually do. The middle layer is interpretation, so it carries a confidence label — and where the data is thin, that label says so.

Insight

Atta 10kg produced ₹19,740 — 43.3% of all revenue — from 24 bills and 47 bags. With rice it reaches 71.6%. Salt and biscuits together moved 22 units and returned ₹588.

Inference High confidence

This is a staples shop that also happens to sell snacks, not a general store. Its income is genuinely fragile to two SKUs: a week without atta would take out roughly two-fifths of turnover, and no amount of biscuit sales would cover it. The pattern is strong enough, and consistent enough across both months, that it is not an artefact of the short window.

Do this

Never let atta or rice run out — check both every morning before opening. Write down the local price of a 10kg atta bag once a month, because a ₹20 gap next door moves 43.3% of this business.

Insight

Milk 1L appears on 22 bills — the second most of any product — and accounts for 59 units, but only ₹3,894 (8.6%) of revenue. Atta is the mirror image: 47 units, 43.3% of the money.

Inference Moderate confidence

Milk is almost certainly the footfall driver — the reason someone walks in at all — while atta is what they occasionally walk out with. That would make milk worth protecting even though it earns little. I can only infer this, though: every bill in this file has exactly one item, so there is no direct evidence that a milk visit ever turns into a second purchase. That evidence does not exist yet because the register has never recorded a basket.

Do this

Keep milk cheap, fresh and never out of stock — treat it as the cost of getting people through the door, not as a product that should pay for itself. Then start writing more than one item per bill, so that in three months there is finally data on what a milk customer also buys.

Insight

6 of 7 named customers owe money. Sukhwinder Kaur owes ₹1,680 — 78.2% of everything she has ever bought here — and Anil Sethi owes 56.8% of his. ₹3,690 of the ₹4,680 book (78.8%) is atta and rice. Not one bill in the file shows a part-payment.

Inference High confidence

Credit is not being extended as a small convenience on small items — it is being extended on precisely the expensive staples the shop has already paid a supplier for. The absence of any part-payment suggests there is no mechanism for it: the customer either clears the whole thing or stays in the book, and so they stay in the book. That is a system problem, not a character problem — these are repeat customers who spend more per visit (₹494) than walk-ins (₹412).

Do this

Cap a single udhaar bill at ₹500 and take cash on atta and rice — those two rules together remove most of the risk without refusing anybody. Then offer part-payment out loud: "jitna ho sake de dijiye." It is the difference between a customer who pays something this week and one who avoids the shop.

Insight

Of 105 bills, the written weekday matches the written date only 20 times. Recomputed from the dates, takings are highest on Thu (₹11,021) and lowest on Sat (₹3,120).

Inference Low confidence

I would not act on the weekday pattern. 48 trading days leaves about seven observations per weekday, and because one atta bill is worth fourteen salt bills, a single large sale moves a whole weekday. Thu looks strong mainly because big-ticket bills happened to land there. What I am confident about is the meta-finding: a whole column of this register is fiction, and it was being written down every single day without anyone checking it against a calendar.

Do this

Stop writing the day by hand — it adds nothing a date does not already contain, and here it added 105 chances to be wrong. Revisit weekday patterns after three to six months of clean records, not before.

Careful — a short period can fool you

August shows ₹25,218 and September ₹20,322, which looks like a fall. It is not safe to call that a decline: September stops on 28 Sep 2026 rather than the 30th, and in a shop where one bag of atta is worth fourteen packets of salt, a handful of big bills landing in one month rather than the next explains the whole gap. Before believing any trend, keep clean records for three to six months.

Proposed tech solution

Khata

One app on the shopkeeper's own phone. Not a billing machine, not a computer, no barcode scanner, nothing to learn. It does one job — it makes the udhaar book impossible to get wrong — and it does it in three taps at the counter.

The problem it solves

₹4,680 is outside the shop with no due date, no balance and no part-payment. Separately, the register mis-states the shop's own sales by ₹1,526. Both have the same root cause: a human being writes every field by hand, at a busy counter, with no constraints.

How it works

1Tap the product from a grid of 10 tiles. Nothing is typed, so there is no 36-spellings problem.
2The price fills itself from the price list. The total is calculated, never written — so it can never be wrong.
3Tap the quantity. Date and weekday are stamped by the phone, so five date formats become one.
4Tap Paid or Udhaar — and if paid, cash or UPI. Method and status stop being one confused column.

Roughly four seconds. That matters, because at a busy counter anything slower simply will not be done.

Start with udhaar only

Almost every sale here is cash, so nothing can be captured automatically — someone has to record it. But for this problem, that is not needed.

A cash sale that is paid on the spot is already finished; there is nothing left to track. Only the udhaar bills need entering — and there were 14 of those in two months, about one every 3.4 trading days. That is the version to start with, because it asks for almost no discipline. Recording every sale is the upgrade later, once the habit exists and the owner can see what it gives him.

The reminder, and why it is calm

The risk with any reminder feature is obvious: nag a neighbour over ₹700 and lose them for good. So the design deliberately gives up some collection speed to protect the relationship.

— Kirana Store Drafted by the app · sent only when the owner taps send
  • The owner presses send, not the app. It writes the message and puts it in a list; he taps send, or skip. If he knows someone has had a bad month, he skips. Automation drafts — the human decides.
  • One message per person per fortnight, maximum. Never daily. The app refuses to send a second one even if asked.
  • It is a statement, not a demand, and it is itemised. People argue with a total and accept a list.
  • It offers part-payment in the message itself — "thoda-thoda bhi chalega" — so the ask is small enough to say yes to.
  • A thank-you goes out when it is paid. Shops remember to ask and forget to thank.

The three other things it does

Bill cap warning — at the counter, before the goods leave

Prevention, not collection

When Udhaar is tapped, the screen shows that person's running balance and warns if this bill would cross ₹500. Bill 5040 — 4 bags of atta, ₹1,680, on its own 35.9% of the entire credit book — would have triggered this. A reminder can only chase money after the goods are gone; this stops it leaving.

Part payment — "thoda-thoda"

Not one part-payment in 105 bills

Today udhaar is all-or-nothing: the register can only record a debt as open or closed, so ₹200 against ₹1,680 has nowhere to go and simply is not collected. A part-payment button with a running balance changes the question from "can you pay ₹1,680?" to "kitna de sakte hain aaj?" — which is a question people answer instead of avoiding. This is the single most effective collection mechanic available here, and it is also the gentlest.

Sunday summary — one message to the owner

No app to open, nothing to learn

Every Sunday evening the app sends the owner one WhatsApp message: sold this week, received this week, still outstanding, who owes what, and which of atta or rice is running low. It is the whole dashboard reduced to six lines. It exists because a tool that has to be opened is a tool that stops being opened — this one comes to him.

What it needs to run

  • A price list of 10 products. Already derivable from this file — every product holds exactly one price across both months.
  • Customer name and phone number. Not in the data. Phone numbers do not exist anywhere in the register and must be collected — 6 of them to start.
  • Opening balances. The ₹4,680 already owed, from the Numbers tab.
  • An Android phone with WhatsApp. Nothing else. It runs offline and syncs when there is signal.

What it deliberately does not do

  • No stock or inventory tracking. There is no stock column in this data to seed it from, and it would demand a daily count — discipline that a register with a duplicated bill and five date formats has not yet earned. Adding it now would make the tool heavy enough to be abandoned.
  • No profit reporting. Not until a purchase-price column exists. A profit number built on selling price alone would be invented.
  • No automatic sending. Deliberately. See above.
  • No requirement to record cash sales. Not at first. The udhaar bills alone — about one every 3.4 trading days — are enough to solve the problem it is built for.
