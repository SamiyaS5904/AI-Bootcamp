The second half of the month looks 10% lower than the first, but that is only where the rice orders happened to land. Twenty-two days is far too short to read a trend, a festival or a best weekday.

Where the Rs 22,885 went

Collected — Rs 17,520 Given on udhaar — Rs 4,315 Never written down — Rs 1,050

The grey piece matters as much as the red one. Three orders have no payment status at all, so the shop cannot say whether that money ever arrived.

Sales and cash are not the same thing

A sale happens the moment goods leave the shop. Cash happens only when the customer pays. Udhaar puts a gap between the two — which is how a shop can look busy all month and still find the cash box empty.

What earns the money

Ten products. Three of them carry the shop, and the two that move fastest across the counter barely register.

One product is holding up the whole shop

Rice sells once or twice a week, so a single missed sale is felt immediately. If rice runs out for a week, or a shop nearby prices the same bag Rs 50 lower, about a third of the business is exposed.

Never let rice, atta or ghee finish. Check their stock daily and the local rice price monthly.

The busiest goods earn the least

Grey is share of items handed over the counter. Dark is share of money earned.

Shelf space should follow value, not speed

Salt and biscuits are worth keeping — they bring people through the door. But they should not be eating the best shelf space or the stock money.

Keep cheap fast-movers near the counter; put rice, atta and ghee where they are seen.

The 80–20 rule

In almost every shop a few items bring most of the money — roughly 20% of products give about 80% of earnings. Here it is sharper: three products out of ten give 65%. That tells you what must never run out, and how much rests on it.

Who pays, and who does not

All nine customers come back. But spending and paying are two different rankings, and only one of them puts money in the box.

Green is paid, red is udhaar, grey is orders where nobody wrote anything down.

The customer who comes most often pays the least

Visits are easy to notice; cash received is what the shop lives on. Ranked by money actually in hand, the quiet customer beats the frequent one four times over.

Count your best customers by what they have paid, and look after the top three — they are 58% of the money.

Working money

Money in a shop moves in a circle: buy stock, sell it, cash returns, buy again. Udhaar breaks the circle — the goods are gone, the cash has not come back, and the supplier still has to be paid.

Two flags

Both are supported by the numbers on the other tabs. Neither costs money to fix.

Udhaar is uncontrolled, and unrecorded

Rs 5,365 — 23.4% of sales — about five days of business

Goods worth Rs 4,315 went out on credit and another Rs 1,050 has nothing written about payment at all. The shop has already paid its supplier for those goods. Eight of the nine customers take udhaar, and Ramesh Kumar alone owes Rs 2,215 — more than half the credit book.

The deeper problem is that the register has no column for repayment. There is no way to tell what has quietly been settled from what is genuinely outstanding. It also worsens under pressure: in Week 2, the weakest week of the month, 42% of sales went on credit, and the largest single credit item was a Rs 1,450 rice bag.

What to do. Start one udhaar notebook this week: date, name, amount, due date, date paid, balance left. Set a limit of about Rs 500 per customer — twelve of the thirteen credit orders were already below it, so it costs no goodwill and stops only the rice bag and Ramesh Kumar's build-up. Take cash for rice and ghee. Agree small monthly payments with Ramesh: keep the customer, change the terms.

The register cannot be trusted

Rs 2,155 of sales missing from the book — 9.4% understated

Six orders have no amount written. One total is wrong by Rs 80. Three orders do not record whether the customer paid. Three customers appear under two spellings each, and dates come in five formats. The owner is therefore working from a sales figure about 9% too low, cannot say who owes what, and cannot even search for a customer by name.

What to do. One date format, one spelling per customer, and never an empty amount or payment box — draw the columns first if it is a paper book. Then add one more column: what the shop paid the supplier. That single column is the difference between knowing what you sell and knowing what you earn.
