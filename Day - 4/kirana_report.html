Insights and inference

Each insight states the claim, shows the evidence that established it, then draws the business inference. Every figure is computed from the cleaned dataset when this page is built, so nothing here can drift out of step with the data.

Insight 1

Credit is a relationship, not a transaction

Evidence
customer typePaid on the spotOn credit
Anonymous walk-in770
Named person1414

Every bill in the file, cross-tabulated by whether a customer name was recorded. The separation is total: not one of the 77 anonymous sales went on credit, while 14 of the 28 named-customer sales did.

Business inference

The causation runs the way it would in any real shop: the shopkeeper writes a name down because he is extending credit, not the other way round. So the credit book is the shop's entire risk surface, and that risk is purely a function of who the customer is. Analytically this also means customer_name and is_paid are not independent variables — any model using both is using one fact twice.

Atta and Rice alone are 72% of revenue; the top three reach 80%. Milk inverts the pattern — 21.0% of bills but only 8.6% of revenue.

Business inference

The two rankings invert, and that inversion is the finding. Staples carry the margin; milk, sugar and salt bring people through the door. They are doing two different jobs and deserve two different stocking rules — a stock-out on Atta costs revenue directly, whereas a stock-out on Milk costs the visit during which Atta would have been bought. Judging products on a single metric hides this completely.

Insight 4

70% of revenue has no name attached to it

Evidence
  • Named regulars: 7 people, 28 bills, ₹13,833 (30% of revenue)
  • Anonymous walk-ins: 77 bills, ₹31,707 (70% of revenue)
  • Mean bill: ₹494 named vs ₹412 anonymous
Business inference

This is a hard ceiling on anything relationship-based. A loyalty scheme, a targeted offer or a churn analysis can only ever reach the 30% of revenue that has an identity attached. It also reframes insight 1: the shop is not choosing to record only some customers, it is recording precisely the ones where it carries risk. Raising the named share would require giving walk-ins a reason to identify themselves.

Insight 5

Price uniquely identifies the product, so the cleanest column contains the messiest one

Evidence
  • 10 distinct prices, 10 distinct products — verified one-to-one in both directions
  • Every price maps to exactly one product: True
  • Every product has exactly one price: True
  • rate was the only column in the raw file needing no cleaning at all, while item used 36 different spellings for those same 10 products
Business inference

Because price determines product, rate alone reconstructs product for all 105 rows — which makes item, the messiest column in the file, strictly redundant. The wider lesson: you cannot judge a column's value by how dirty it looks. Of the two worst columns here, customer proved the most valuable in the dataset and item proved worth nothing that was not already available elsewhere. Only testing what a column relates to tells them apart.

How the data was cleaned

Eight decisions, each licensed by a test rather than by habit. The right-hand column names the test.

decisionthe test that licensed it
Dropped duplicated bill 5016Identical across all 10 columns, whereas a real two-item bill would differ on item. Left in, it would have inflated revenue by ₹700.
Stripped Rs, ₹, /-, pcs and paddingThe values were correct and only the types were wrong, which makes this a formatting fix rather than a judgement call.
Derived 4 quantities and 8 amountsamount = qty × rate held on 93 of 93 checkable rows with zero mismatches, so these rebuild exactly. Mean-imputing would have invented money.
Mapped 36 item names onto 10 productsThe variation is linguistic, not typographic — Chawal is Rice, Doodh is Milk, Parle-G is a biscuit. No string function collapses those.
Split payment_status into is_paid + payment_methodOne column was answering two questions. 'Cash' is a payment method, not a third payment state.
Normalised 22 customer strings to 7 regulars plus a walk-in flagCase variants, invisible trailing whitespace, and four different spellings of 'no name recorded'.
Resolved dates per row against the Aug–Sep windowFour formats coexist. dayfirst=True would have silently mis-parsed the 8 month-first rows; a single format= string fails outright.
Dropped the day columnNot dirty but fabricated — 19% agreement with the real weekday against 14% by chance. The safest cleaning action was deletion.

What this data cannot answer

The limits matter as much as the findings. Each question below is unanswerable from this file, and no amount of cleaning changes that.

What do customers buy together?

Every one of the 105 bills contains exactly one product. There are no baskets in this file, so market-basket and affinity analysis are impossible — not difficult, impossible. Cleaning cannot create data that was never recorded.

How does demand respond to price?

Each product held exactly one price for the entire period. With no variation in the input there is nothing to correlate against, so elasticity is unanswerable by construction.

Which weekday is busiest?

The day column was fabricated and dropped. Weekday can be recomputed from the resolved dates, but at roughly 1.7 bills per day the counts are far too thin to support a claim about weekly rhythm.

Is trade growing or shrinking?

The ledger spans 02 Aug to 28 Sep, so the first and last calendar weeks are partial buckets holding 3 and 1 bills. A weekly revenue chart would show a dramatic collapse that is purely a calendar artefact — which is why this report does not include one.

When exactly did bills 5036 and 5096 happen?

Both dates are ambiguous between two readings that fall inside the ledger window, so the file cannot say which is correct. Both rows carry date_ambiguous = true, and the earlier reading was taken as an arbitrary tie-break.

Methodology note

This dataset appears to be generated rather than recorded. Three observations point the same way: every product held one price to the rupee across two months; amount equalled qty × rate on all 93 checkable rows without a single arithmetic slip; and every unpaid bill received exactly zero rather than a part-payment. A hand-kept ledger does none of those three things — prices drift, sums get fumbled, and customers pay half now and the rest on Friday.

That does not weaken the analysis, but it does bound what may be claimed from it. The cleaning and validation technique used here transfers to real data completely. The business conclusions do not transfer to any actual shop — “milk drives footfall” is a pattern in generated numbers, not evidence about a real trader.

Stated plainly, because an analysis that hides this reads as more authoritative than it has earned.
